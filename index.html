THE CLIMB

A READING OF “THE WOMAN AT THE TOP OF THE WORLD”
STORY BY MATTHEW J. KIRBY
REFLECTION BY JUDE

“the irony of Able’s name was not lost on him”

▼ SCROLL TO CLIMB

LEVEL 1-1 · THE VILLAGE

“But all his name did was remind Able of what he wasn’t.”MATTHEW J. KIRBY

Able is named for the one thing he has never been allowed to be. Kirby stacks the odds before the story has even moved: a leg like “a little twig”, a village of boys who went out to the fields years ago, and a name that works like an insult he has to answer to.

His parents “had used his name to love him, anyway.” The village uses it to measure what he isn’t — and on the first page, the reader is quietly invited to agree with the village.

LEVEL 1-2 · HIS MOTHER

“Even were you with them, there’d be no work for you. This drought has made sure of that.”ABLE’S MOTHER

This is the kindest sentence in the story and it still closes a door. She has just thanked him for his strong hands and arms — the parts of him that work — and then, gently, told him it wouldn’t matter anyway.

Two impossibilities are now stacked on one boy: his body, and the land. Neither is his fault, and neither can be argued with.

LEVEL 1-3 · THE DECISION

“Three fools have gone up the mountain. None reached the peak, and all three came back empty-handed.”ABLE’S FATHER

Whole men tried and failed. That is the measure Able is handed the night before he goes — not encouragement, but proof that it can’t be done by people more able than him.

He goes anyway. Kirby writes that “there were times when belief in a story was no longer a choice, and everyone became a fool.” This is the exact moment hope stops being the reason and grit takes over.

LEVEL 1-4 · THE GAUNTLET

“Somehow, this is your fault. Curses beget curses.”A MAN IN THE VILLAGE
“But all that fell on Able’s back were words.”MATTHEW J. KIRBY

Before the mountain there is the village. He could have taken another road, but it would cost time and distance he cannot spare — so he walks straight through the hatred with his crutch in full view.

That second line is the first real evidence of what he is. The shouting lands. It just doesn’t stop him.

LEVEL 2-1 · THE FOOTHILLS

“a dull ache of protest at being asked to do more than it was ever meant to”ABLE’S LEG
“But he climbed. And climbed.”MATTHEW J. KIRBY

Kirby gives the endurance its own paragraph, two words at a time, because that is how endurance actually happens — not in one heroic burst but in repetitions that stop being interesting.

The hip is the worst of it: bones that “had never fully joined”, grinding against each other with every step. Each step becomes “a decision he had to make and from which he had to rest.”

LEVEL 2-2 · SECOND DAWN

“he struggled to his feet” … “something as small as his leg, something that had amounted to so little, could so overwhelm his body and mind”MATTHEW J. KIRBY

The mountain has already taken a night from him. He has no food and no water — he sucks dew off “bitter, dusty pine needles” — and he wakes into pain before the sun is up.

Then he grabs his crutch and turns uphill. Grit here isn’t strength; it is the willingness to start again in worse condition than yesterday.

LEVEL 2-3 · THE GRANITE WALL

“Able looked at his crutch, which his father had carved for him, and then tossed it away. Then he took hold of the mountain by the folds in its skin and climbed.”MATTHEW J. KIRBY

This is the hinge of the whole story. The crutch is the only object in it made out of love — his father carved it — and to go any higher he has to throw it off a mountain.

Kirby chooses the word skin: the wall that defeated everyone before him becomes something living, something that can be held. From here Able climbs on arms alone, a hundred feet up, until “they quivered dangerously.”

LEVEL 2-4 · HIS LIFE AT HIS FINGERTIPS

“He was stranded, too weak to continue, yet unable to rest, a spider climbing glass.”MATTHEW J. KIRBY
“The world pivoted, he swung, but maintained his grasp on the rock with his right hand, his life at his fingertips.”MATTHEW J. KIRBY

The climax of the story is not the summit — it is this. One hand, no foothold, fingers already bleeding from under the nails, and a fatal drop underneath.

And what he does is close his eyes, breathe, and assess. That is the story’s real definition of grit: not the absence of fear, but the refusal to let fear make the decision.

LEVEL 3-1 · THE WOMAN AT THE TOP OF THE WORLD

“Why’ve you come? Who are you?”
“I am Able,” he said.
“That … is a good answer.”THE WITCH, AND ABLE

He is asked for the name that has meant what he isn’t for fourteen years, and he gives it as a plain fact. The witch calls it a good answer before she mends anything.

Then: “Your leg is mended. A sturdy branch for you.” The leg is the reward. The sentence he said in the dark is the proof he had already earned it.

LEVEL 3-2 · THE RAIN

“through the tattered remains of his trousers he saw a strong, healthy leg. An able leg.”MATTHEW J. KIRBY
“With each note, clouds gathered and multiplied … When the clouds let loose their rain on the mountainside, and the pastures, and the fields below, he wept.”MATTHEW J. KIRBY

He gets the leg he climbed for — and immediately it isn’t the point. “But that was not why he had come.” The drought is still there and the witch is gone, and he believes he has failed.

What lifts the flute to his lips is thinking of his mum and da. Grit gets him to the top; love decides what he does once he’s there.

LEVEL 3-3 · THE COST

“The witch had gone, but Able heard the music now, and he would stay here at the top of the world to play it.”MATTHEW J. KIRBY

It is worth noticing that this victory never comes home. He saves the village and does not walk back into it. He takes the old woman’s place — her post, her flute, and her solitude at the top of the world.

Kirby lets him win and still asks what winning took. The last line of the story is not about the rain: “Perhaps they would even hear his name.” After everything, what he wants is for his parents to hear him called Able and have it finally be true.

★ THE FULL SAYING ★

The prompt asked for the best saying of wisdom in this story. A single sentence can’t carry it. Read the climb back in order and the meaning is the sequence, not any one line:

  1. 1-1“all his name did was remind Able of what he wasn’t”
  2. 1-2“there’d be no work for you. This drought has made sure of that”
  3. 1-3“Three fools have gone up the mountain. None reached the peak”
  4. 1-4“But all that fell on Able’s back were words”
  5. 2-1“But he climbed. And climbed.”
  6. 2-2“he struggled to his feet”
  7. 2-3“tossed it away … took hold of the mountain by the folds in its skin”
  8. 2-4“his life at his fingertips”
  9. 3-1“I am Able.” — “That … is a good answer.”
  10. 3-2“An able leg.” … “he wept”
  11. 3-3“Perhaps they would even hear his name.”

No matter the odds, as long as you have grit and a belief in yourself that surpasses others’, you can do anything — from taking your first steps outside, to summiting a mountain, all with a broken leg.

The impossible is never really impossible. The sky is not the limit.

As long as you have grit by your side, you can also be the man or woman at the top of the world.

“The Woman at the Top of the World” is a short story by Matthew J. Kirby.
